Researching the Game AI Industry: Roles, Studios, and Portfolios

Before writing a single CV bullet, I researched the market I'm aiming at — the roles, the studios, and what a portfolio here actually has to prove.

Industry Research Game AI Careers Portfolio Design

I wanted the evidence to shape my materials, not the other way round. So I researched the part of the industry I'm targeting first, then built my CV, cover letter, and portfolio around what it values.

The roles

"Game AI" is two jobs under one name. Traditional gameplay AI — behaviour trees, blackboards, state machines, AI pathfinding — and the newer generative layer of LLM dialogue and procedural content. Current AI Programmer listings (e.g. Great Ape Games, Unreal, remote) still lead with the classical set: behaviour trees, pathfinding, and "solid C++." The baseline repeats everywhere — C++ and C#, Unreal or Unity, Python for ML. Generative AI is now an expected competency, but it sits on top of those foundations. That corrected my instinct to lean only on the LLM angle.

The studios

I mapped where LLM-driven characters — what my own project explores — are built in production:

  • Ubisoft's NEO NPC — a ~30-person R&D team across Paris, Montpellier and Montreal, with Inworld AI and NVIDIA Audio2Face; still an internal playtest, not shipped.
  • Rockstar — patents on "dialogue decay" and "personality drift modelling," attacking character coherence over time.
  • Epic — Fortnite AI NPCs and Unreal 5 AI tooling; Krafton's inZOI ships on-device AI characters via NVIDIA ACE.
  • Middleware — Inworld, Convai and ACE are what studios integrate, so tooling firms are employers too.

The pattern: outside a few indies, AAA studios haven't handed real dialogue to AI. The work is R&D and tooling — building systems that make generative characters safe, cheap and coherent enough to ship.

The hard problems — and my project

The industry's open problems are the ones my own work tackles. Coherence: Inworld describes "narrative controls" keeping characters "grounded in the information the narrative team provides"; Rockstar's patents fight the same drift. My Constraint Resolution Engine does exactly this — grounding replies in a structured character and keeping key beats authored, not generated. Cost: every generated line is billed per token, so studios cap AI turns. Building on a metered API, I scripted the load-bearing moments and reserved generation for open chat — the same trade-off, solved the same way.

Portfolios

For AI programming roles, a portfolio isn't screenshots — it's evidence of systems you built and can explain: behaviour trees and decision systems, readable GitHub history, and a clear account of the problem solved. For the generative angle, the differentiator is showing you've grappled with coherence, grounding and cost, not just wired an API to a chat box.

What changed

The research sharpened all three documents. The CV now foregrounds the C#/engine/systems baseline instead of assuming the AI angle carries it. The cover letter targets the roles that exist — systems and tooling around bounded AI features — not a fantasy "AI storyteller" job. The portfolio presents my CRE project in the industry's own terms: coherence, grounding, cost. My positioning as a software engineer with an interest in AI, through the lens of game development, is now anchored to what studios actually hire for.
